# Model governance

Three predictive models are in production in this system. This document states,
for each, what it is for, what it was trained on, how it performs, where it
should not be used, and what would have to be monitored if it were real.

> **All data and model outputs in this demonstration are synthetic and are not
> intended for real-world financial, credit, or customer decisions.**

---

## 1. Purpose, and what each model is not

| Model | Predicts | Explicitly not |
|---|---|---|
| **Attrition risk** | Probability a customer becomes behaviourally inactive over the next three months | A statement that the customer will leave, or a licence to act without checking why |
| **Investment propensity** | Probability an eligible customer takes an investment or savings product next period | A suitability assessment. Interest is not suitability, and no product may be discussed on this basis alone |
| **Lending propensity** | Probability an eligible customer takes a lending product next period | A credit decision, an affordability assessment, or an input to either |

The third row is the one that matters most. A model that ranks customers by
likelihood of *taking* credit is not a model of whether credit *should* be
extended, and the two must never be confused. The decision engine enforces this
structurally: any customer in arrears is routed to collections support and every
commercial action is suppressed, regardless of how attractive their scores are.

## 2. Training data

- **Source**: synthetic, generated by `src/customer_intelligence/data_generation/`
  from a known latent-trait process. 50,000 customers over 15 months.
- **Feature window**: months 1–12.
- **Outcome window**: months 13–15.
- **Split**: 75/25 stratified train/test, fixed seed.
- **Preprocessing**: median imputation and standardisation for numeric inputs;
  most-frequent imputation and one-hot encoding for categorical, with categories
  below 1% frequency grouped and unseen categories ignored at scoring time.

Features are declared in one place — `analytics/features.py` — so "what went into
this model?" is answered by reading a single list rather than tracing a notebook.

## 3. Target definitions

**Attrition.** A customer is churned if, across the three-month outcome window,
they made fewer than two transactions *and* their monthly logins fell below 25%
of their feature-window average. Two conditions, because a single quiet month is
not attrition.

This is a *behavioural* definition, and its consequences should be stated
plainly. It is easier to predict than a commercial definition (account closure,
balance transferred out), because the behaviour it measures begins inside the
feature window and continues into the outcome window. **The ROC-AUC reported
below is therefore not comparable to a churn model built on a commercial
definition, and should not be read as evidence that this modelling approach would
reach that figure on a real book.** It is the label that is easy, not the model
that is good.

**Product uptake.** A customer took a product if a holding of that type started
inside the outcome window, given they did not hold it at the close of the feature
window.

## 4. Eligible populations

Each model is trained only on customers who could have experienced its outcome,
and refuses to score anyone outside that population.

| Model | Eligible | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Attrition | Customers with ≥ 3 transactions in the feature window | Scoring attrition for a customer who was never active measures nothing |
| Investment propensity | Customers not already holding an investment product | Otherwise the model learns "does not already hold it", which is eligibility, not propensity |
| Lending propensity | Customers not already holding a lending product | Same |

Scores are set to null, not zero, for ineligible customers. Zero is a
prediction; null is an admission that the model has nothing to say. The
application prints "Already held" rather than a number.

## 5. Performance

Measured on held-out data. Figures are regenerated on every build and written to
`models/model_cards.json`; the application reads them there rather than
reproducing them by hand.

| Model | ROC-AUC | PR-AUC | Base rate | Recall at threshold | Lift |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Attrition risk | ~0.92 | ~0.75 | ~11% | ~0.67 | ~6.6× |
| Investment propensity | ~0.83 | ~0.28 | ~5% | ~0.57 | ~3.8× |
| Lending propensity | ~0.67 | ~0.20 | ~11% | ~0.30 | ~2.0× |

**On the lending model.** ROC-AUC 0.67 is modest, and it is reported rather than
quietly dropped. Credit appetite is largely unobservable from the behaviour
available before a customer borrows; the model finds a real but weak signal in
balance-to-income and outflow patterns, and doubles the base rate at the
operating threshold. That is genuinely useful for prioritising a finite
outreach list and genuinely insufficient for anything else, and the honest
report is what allows that distinction to be made.

**On the calibration.** Discrimination is not the whole story. A model used to
decide who gets contacted must be right about the *level*, not only the order —
"when it says 20%, does 20% happen". Reliability by decile is shown on the
**Data quality & governance** page for all three models.

## 6. Model selection

Logistic regression, random forest and gradient boosting are all trained on every
target and compared on held-out data. The comparison table is published in the
application.

**Logistic regression ships**, even where gradient boosting scores marginally
higher. The reason is on the Decision support page: every recommendation carries
a per-customer explanation built from standardised coefficients times the
customer's distance from the book average. That is the arithmetic of the model's
decision for that row — readable, additive, and checkable by hand. A small gain
in discrimination did not justify losing it.

This is a judgement, not a rule, and the comparison table is published precisely
so it can be made on evidence. If the gap were large — several points of AUC on a
decision with real cost — the trade-off should be revisited.

## 7. Explainability

| Method | Where used | What it answers |
|---|---|---|
| Standardised coefficients | Model card | Which inputs move the score, and in which direction, across the population |
| Permutation importance | Model card | How far performance falls when one input is shuffled — what the model *relies on* |
| Coefficient × z-score | Per customer, Decision support | Why *this* customer scored as they did |

SHAP is not used. For a linear model it would reproduce the coefficient
contribution already shown, with an extra dependency and a less direct
explanation. It would earn its place only alongside a model that needed it.

**Importance is not causation.** Permutation importance measures what the model
depends on, which is not the same as what causes attrition. Nothing in this
system licenses a causal claim, and the application says so where the chart
appears.

## 8. Protected attributes and fairness

`gender` is present in the source data and shown in the customer profile, because
a real extract would contain it. **It is not an input to any model.**

"It was in the data" is not a reason to let a commercial targeting model
condition on a protected attribute. If the model found gender predictive, that
would be the problem, not the justification — the system would then be allocating
retention effort and product offers by gender. It is retained for outcome
*monitoring*: checking whether flag rates and recommended actions differ across
groups, which is the use that justifies holding the attribute at all.

`tests/test_leakage.py::test_gender_is_not_a_model_feature` enforces this, so a
future edit that re-introduced it would fail the suite rather than pass silently.

Other attributes deserve scrutiny that this demonstration does not fully
discharge. `region`, `employment_type` and `income_band` are model inputs and can
proxy for characteristics that should not drive treatment. In a real deployment
the minimum would be: flag rates and precision reported per group, a stated
tolerance for divergence, and a named owner for the decision when it is exceeded.

## 9. Leakage controls

The structural risk in a system like this is that a feature quietly reaches into
the outcome window, producing a model that validates beautifully and fails in
production.

1. Training and scoring features are produced by **the same SQL file** with a
   different `analysis_window`, so the two definitions cannot diverge.
2. Product holdings are filtered by `start_month_index <= window_to`, so a
   product taken in the outcome window is never counted as held.
3. `declared_segment` is excluded: it is a rule applied to the same attributes
   the model already sees, and including it would make the explanations circular.
4. Each propensity model is blind to whether the customer holds its target
   product.
5. `tests/test_leakage.py` builds the 360 over two windows and asserts the
   results differ in the right direction, and greps the SQL for any month literal
   reaching into the outcome window.

## 10. Thresholds

The operating threshold is **not 0.5**. With base rates near 5–11%, a 0.5 cut-off
flags almost nobody.

Thresholds are set so the model flags a fixed share of the eligible book: 10% for
attrition, 15% for each propensity model. This is a **capacity decision** — how
many customers a team can actually contact — declared in
`analytics/models.py::CAPACITY_SHARE` so it can be argued with and changed
deliberately. Changing the capacity assumption changes the threshold; it does not
change the model.

## 11. Monitoring, if this were real

| Signal | Why | Action if it moves |
|---|---|---|
| Feature distribution stability | Inputs drift before outputs do | Investigate the source system before the model |
| Calibration by decile | The model can keep its ranking while losing its level | Recalibrate; ranking-based metrics will not show this |
| Precision at the operating threshold | The number that determines whether outreach is worth doing | Re-examine the threshold and the capacity assumption |
| Share of the book flagged | A model flagging 30% has changed, whatever its AUC says | Halt and investigate |
| Flag rate by group | Divergence is a fairness issue whether or not it was intended | Escalate to the named owner |
| Action mix from the decision engine | Rules can shift the effort mix without any model changing | Review the rule ordering |

## 12. Limitations

1. **The data is synthetic** and generated from a known process. Relationships
   are cleaner than reality; there are no seasonal effects, no macroeconomic
   shocks, no data migrations mid-panel.
2. **Performance figures are a property of that process** and are not a forecast
   of performance on a real portfolio.
3. **The attrition label is behavioural, not commercial** — see §3. This is the
   single largest reason the reported AUC is high.
4. **Fifteen months is a short panel.** No model here has seen a full annual
   cycle, so nothing seasonal could have been learned.
5. **The opportunity score is a heuristic**, not a fitted model, and not a
   revenue forecast. There is no ground truth for "opportunity"; a model that
   appeared to find one would be fitting to whatever the organisation happened to
   sell last year.
6. **The decision engine encodes one set of policy assumptions.** A different
   organisation would order the rules differently, and should.
7. **No causal claim is supported anywhere.** Every relationship in this system
   is associational.

## 13. Human oversight

The system produces suggested actions. It decides nothing.

Every recommendation names the rule that fired, the evidence beneath it, the
constraints on acting, and a confidence grade based on how much history exists
for that customer — not on how extreme the score is. The language throughout is
"suggested action", never "approve" or "grant". Doing nothing is a reachable
outcome of the rule set and is recorded as a decision, because an engine that
always finds something to do is generating contact rather than insight.
